*Portal 2* gives you one tool. It fires two linked openings onto flat white surfaces, and whatever goes into one comes out of the other with its speed intact. Everything new arrives as a feature of the room, and the campaign's real achievement is how it gets a player to understand each of those features without ever stopping to explain.

## Introduce, test, combine

Almost every mechanic follows the same three steps.

First it is **introduced** where nothing can go wrong. The first Aerial Faith Plate, a catapult set into the floor, simply throws you across a gap onto a safe ledge. You cannot miss and nothing is chasing you.

Then it is **tested** alone. A later room asks a single question about the new thing: the plate throws you towards a wall panel, and you have to realise that a portal there turns the launch into a route somewhere else.

Then it is **combined**. A few chambers on, the plate is one part of a machine that also includes a laser, a cube and a button, and the puzzle is about the order of operations. The game has stopped teaching the plate. It assumes you own it.

The pattern repeats for each element, and new ideas almost never arrive two at a time.

| Mechanic | Where it is introduced | What it later combines with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Laser and redirection cube | Early chambers under GLaDOS | Portals, laser relays, turrets |
| Aerial Faith Plate | Early chambers under GLaDOS | Portals on angled panels, cubes in flight |
| Hard Light Bridge | Later GLaDOS chambers | Portals that carry the bridge, turrets (as a shield) |
| Repulsion Gel (blue) | Old Aperture, the 1950s shafts | Momentum jumps, gel-coated cubes |
| Propulsion Gel (orange) | Old Aperture, a later era | Repulsion Gel, long portal flings |
| Conversion Gel (white) | Old Aperture, the last era | Everything, since it creates portal surfaces |
| Excursion Funnel | Wheatley's chambers | Gel carried in the beam, faith plates, turrets |

## Rooms that narrow the question

The original *Portal* sometimes asked for quick reflexes. The sequel mostly does not. Its chambers are big and dramatic, but only a few surfaces accept a portal, often a handful of white panels in a hall of dark metal. That restriction is the design. With only a few places a portal can go, the difficulty moves out of the hands and into the head. You are not performing the solution, you are trying to see it.

The rooms also direct attention quietly: important panels are lit, a pipe dripping gel draws the eye upward. The exit is usually visible from the entrance, so you know the goal and only lack the route. I almost never felt lost, only temporarily stupid, which is the feeling a puzzle game should aim for.

## Story on the same schedule

The writing runs on the same schedule as the teaching. The three gels are introduced during a long climb through the abandoned lower levels of Aperture Science, and each belongs to a different decade of the company's decline, narrated by recordings of its founder, Cave Johnson. You learn a mechanic and a piece of history in the same room. Later, when the dim-witted Wheatley takes over the facility, his test chambers are clumsy on purpose, and the joke only lands because by then you can tell a well made chamber from a bad one. The game has trained your taste along with your skills.

:::details[Spoiler for the ending]
The final move is the best example of the whole method. For hours you have been taught one rule above all others: white surfaces take portals. Cave Johnson mentions in passing that the white gel is made from ground-up moon rock. In the last seconds of the final fight the roof opens, the moon is visible, and the game waits to see whether you will put the two facts together. A punchline and a final exam at once.
:::

## What is less good

The middle act has long stretches of walking between test shafts, hunting for one distant white wall. And because the puzzles are built to be *seen* rather than performed, a solved chamber has little left to offer a second time. The separate co-operative campaign helps: two players, four portals, and puzzles that need timing and trust between partners.

These are small complaints about a game that respects its player more than almost any other. It never hands out the answer, and it never asks a question it has not prepared you for.
