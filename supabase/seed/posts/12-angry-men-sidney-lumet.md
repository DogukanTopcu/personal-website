A jury of twelve retires to decide whether an eighteen-year-old killed his father. Eleven vote guilty almost at once. One man says he is not sure, and would like to talk. That is the whole premise, and for the next hour and a half the film barely leaves the room. It should feel like a recorded play. Instead it is one of the most carefully *directed* films of the 1950s, and most of what makes it work is invisible the first time through.

## The room gets smaller

This was Sidney Lumet's first feature, shot by Boris Kaufman, and together they built a camera plan that runs under the whole picture. Lumet described it years later in his book *Making Movies*. The first third is filmed from above eye level, with wider lenses, so you see the table, the walls and the space between the men. The middle third comes down to eye level. The last third is shot from below, and by then the lenses have grown steadily longer.

A long lens flattens depth. Faces fill the frame, the background presses up behind them, and the ceiling begins to appear at the top of the shot. Nobody thinks "the focal length has changed". They feel that a room which looked large enough at the start now has no air in it. The weather does the rest: shirts darken with sweat, a fan refuses to work, a storm breaks outside and the light in the room changes with it.

Then comes the release. When the jurors finally walk out onto the courthouse steps, Lumet uses the widest lens in the film and lifts the camera high. After ninety minutes of tightening, the sudden space is felt physically. It is one of the best final shots I know, and almost nothing happens in it.

## Twelve men, one table

Staging a dozen people around a table is a geography problem. Lumet solves it at the start with a long unbroken take as the men drift in. The camera wanders from group to group, picks up scraps of small talk, and by the time everyone sits down you know the layout and have a first impression of each juror, without a single introduction. None of them is named until the final minute, and then only two. You learn them by seat, voice and temperament.

After that the blocking carries the argument. Characters get up when they are losing, and walk to the window or the water cooler to regroup. Henry Fonda's Juror 8 begins the film alone at the window with his back to the room, and the staging keeps him apart until others start to move towards him.

The strongest example comes late. One juror launches into a bigoted tirade, and as he goes on, the others stand up one at a time, leave the table, and turn their backs. Nobody argues with him. The composition does it: a man talking to an empty table in a room full of people. The film never states its verdict on him because the blocking already has.

## A script built like a staircase

Reginald Rose adapted the screenplay from his own television play, and its structure is as deliberate as the camera plan. The prosecution's case rests on a few pillars: an unusual knife, an old man who heard the killing through his ceiling, a woman who saw it through the windows of a passing train. The script takes them one at a time. Each gets its own sequence and its own small demonstration (the knife is the most famous), and the sequences are punctuated by votes.

Those votes are the film's clock. Eleven to one becomes ten to two after a secret ballot, and later the count reaches six to six. The tally tells you where you are in the story, and Rose varies the method, aloud, on slips, by a show of hands, so that each vote exposes different people.

What keeps this from being mechanical is that nobody changes sides for the same reason. One man is persuaded by evidence, another out of respect for someone who stood alone, another because he wants to get to a ball game and has stopped caring which way it goes. Juror 8 is often remembered as the hero who knows the boy is innocent. He never says that. His position is only that he does not know, and the film is careful to leave the actual question unanswered.

## Where it creaks

Some of it has aged. The jury is twelve white men, true to the juries of its day and a limit now. A couple of the conversions come quickly, and a juror doing his own detective work outside the courtroom, as one does here, could get a real verdict thrown out. Lee J. Cobb's final breakdown is pitched for the back row.

None of that does much damage. This is a film about doubt that is itself made with total certainty: every lens, seat and line is there on purpose. Anyone interested in how staging can carry an argument should watch it twice, once for the story and once with the sound of the plan ticking underneath.
